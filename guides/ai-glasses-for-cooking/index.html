AI glasses for cooking: which ones actually help.

Cooking is the use case smart glasses were made for: your hands are busy, dirty and holding a knife. Here is what each pair on sale in 2026 does in a real kitchen, and the part all of them still get wrong.

Updated October 1, 2026 By Malik Shourbaji, founder of Glutt 3 min read

Short answer

For cooking, Ray-Ban Meta and Oakley Meta glasses are the practical choice: open-ear audio, a microphone that hears you over a hob, and Meta AI for questions and timers. Meta Ray-Ban Display adds a screen so you can read the next step. Ray-Ban Meta Audio drops the camera and costs less. Solos AirGo offers voice-guided cooking as an alternative. What none of them do on their own is know your recipe, which is why people pair them with a cooking app.

What glasses are genuinely good at in a kitchen

  • Audio that leaves your ears open. You still hear the pan, the timer and the person talking to you. This is the single biggest advantage over earbuds.
  • A microphone close to your mouth, so you can ask something over an extractor fan without shouting.
  • Hands you never have to wipe. No tapping, no unlocking, no greasy screen.
  • A point-of-view camera on most models, for photos of the finished plate and, on some, for asking "does this look done?"

And what they are not good at: choosing what to cook, remembering your recipe, or knowing what is in your fridge. Those are phone jobs.

Camera or no camera, for cooking specifically

The camera matters less than the marketing suggests. With Meta's live AI you can ask about what you are looking at, which is genuinely useful for "is this done?" But third-party cooking apps still cannot use the glasses camera: that runs through Meta's Wearables Device Access Toolkit, which remains a developer preview, with no distribution to end users. 2

So if you want glasses mainly for cooking, the camera-free Ray-Ban Meta Audio loses you very little today: voice guidance from an app works identically, and a phone propped on the counter sees the pan better than your face does anyway.

If you saw "Traeger MEAT-AI grilling glasses" with thermal imaging and night vision: that was an April Fools' gag. Its buy buttons link to Traeger's /april-fools page. 4 No real cooking glasses do thermal imaging.

The gap every pair has

Glasses answer questions from general knowledge. They do not know that you saved a chicken recipe on Tuesday, that you are on step 3, that the timer running belongs to the rice, or that you have no shallots. That is why the setup people settle on is glasses plus a cooking app: the glasses carry the conversation, and the app carries the recipe and your kitchen.

Buying notes

  • Battery beats features for cooking. A long braise plus a live voice session is a lot of radio time.
  • Prescription matters. Meta sells prescription-ready frames; budget for lenses.
  • Steam fogs lenses. Any glasses, not a flaw of these.
  • Check which country you are in. Meta's live AI has rolled out in English in the US and Canada first.

Frequently asked questions

Can AI glasses help with cooking?

Yes, mainly through voice: asking questions with dirty hands, setting timers, and hearing guidance while you keep both hands on the food. Meta AI on Ray-Ban Meta and Oakley Meta glasses does this out of the box, and cooking apps can speak through the glasses too.

What are the best smart glasses for cooking?

Ray-Ban Meta or Oakley Meta for most people, because of open-ear audio and a microphone that works over a noisy hob. Meta Ray-Ban Display if you would rather read the next step than hear it. Ray-Ban Meta Audio if you want the cheapest version of the same voice experience without a camera.

Do smart glasses show recipes in your vision?

Only the ones with a display. Meta Ray-Ban Display can show cards in one lens, and New York Times Cooking is adding swipeable recipe step cards for it. Non-display glasses read steps aloud instead.

Can smart glasses tell if my food is cooked?

Meta AI can comment on what the camera sees, which helps, but it is a visual guess. Use a thermometer for meat: the USDA safe minimum is 165°F for poultry.

Do I need glasses with a camera for cooking?

Not really. Third-party cooking apps cannot use the glasses camera yet, so a camera-free pair plus a phone propped facing the stove gives you the same help for less money.
